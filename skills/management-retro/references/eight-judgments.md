# Eight judgments for a management retrospective

This reference distills the author's essay [管理复盘：从执行到系统的八个判断](https://liyuk.com/writing/2026/08/management-retrospective/). The eight judgments are a reasoning sequence, not eight required headings or a checklist to complete mechanically. Select the lenses that matter to the case.

## 1. Problem judgment

Before asking how execution went, define what outcome was sought and what actually happened. Make the judgment discussable: goal, evidence, constraints, trade-offs, and what observation could falsify the explanation. Different participants may be answering different questions; align the question before resolving disagreement.

## 2. Boundaries and responsibility

Clarify who decides, who executes, when to escalate, and where responsibility ends. A role or title does not prove ownership. Distinguish healthy redundancy (review, backup, resilience) from harmful overlap (competing authority or duplicated commitments). “Ownership” is a bounded commitment, not unlimited availability.

## 3. Alignment

Check whether people share the desired outcome, the relevant facts, and decision rights. Sending information is not the same as enabling a decision; receiving context does not grant authority. Treat disagreement as a question about goals, evidence, or decision rights before treating it as unwillingness.

## 4. Context transfer

Inspect handoffs for loss of why, constraints, and prior decisions. Assign work around deliverable outcomes where feasible; retain the context in an accessible document and close the loop in the receiving workflow. Meetings alone do not preserve context.

## 5. Outcome closure

Separate “task completed” from “intended result verified.” For incidents, protect people and users first: prevent, detect, contain, repair; analyze later when the situation is stable. A fix without follow-up evidence is not confirmed effective.

## 6. Resources and capacity

Locate the gap between the outcome and current capability. It may be skill, process, dependency, system constraint, or headcount; do not turn every gap into a staffing ratio. Avoid double-committing the same people and making chronic overwork invisible. More resources can also create unfocused work if the actual constraint is unclear.

## 7. Team and growth

Look for whether judgment and reliable collaboration are becoming transferable, rather than equating growth with more people or a few heroes. Match stretch scope to current capability and support; do not use “development” to disguise unsupported workload or replace a needed system improvement with individual effort.

## 8. Next-case learning

Return the learning to the next similar choice: what to keep, change, or stop. Prefer a few concrete actions with a verification signal (for example, a dependency check at kickoff, a recorded decision, or a second person able to perform a critical verification). Do not add process without a failure pattern and a way to tell whether the change helped.

## Through-line

Define the problem → clarify boundaries → align facts and expectations → preserve context → verify the outcome → understand the capability gap → help the team carry judgment forward. This order helps analysis; it does not require every response to expose every step.
